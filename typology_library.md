# Typology library

This document is generated from veilpierce/knowledge/typologies.json by the command veilpierce docs. The cards are summaries written for this project. They paraphrase public guidance, they are not quotations and they are not legal advice. The reading references name the documents a reviewer should consult for the authoritative text.

## T01 Professional nominee directors fronting many companies

Area: Nominee director network

A nominee director lends a name to companies that are run by someone else. The nominee satisfies the legal requirement for a natural person on the board while the real controller stays out of the public record.

Indicators

* One natural person holds an unusually large number of directorships
* The companies behind those appointments are dormant, never file accounts or are dissolved within two years
* The nominee is the only director of each company
* The nominee lives in a different country from the stated business
* Appointments cluster at a small number of shared addresses

Why it matters: Volume on its own is not proof. Accountants and professional directors also hold many appointments. The combination of volume with disposable companies and sole directorships is what marks a nominee network.

Reviewer actions

* Request evidence of who gives instructions to the director
* Treat every company fronted by the same nominee as one connected risk
* Apply enhanced due diligence before onboarding or continuing the relationship

Reading references: FATF and Egmont Group, Concealment of Beneficial Ownership (2018); HM Treasury and Home Office, National Risk Assessment of Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing (2020)

## T02 Informal nominees and front persons

Area: Nominee director network

Controllers also use relatives, employees or paid strangers as the declared owner. These front persons appear as the person with significant control but have no means or expertise that would explain the holding.

Indicators

* The declared owner controls several unrelated companies in unrelated sectors
* The declared owner has no visible business history
* The declared owner and the director are both resident overseas
* Control records are filed on the day of incorporation and never updated

Why it matters: A front person makes the register look complete. The absence of a gap is exactly what hides the real controller.

Reviewer actions

* Ask for source of wealth evidence for the declared owner
* Compare the declared owner with signatories and people who give payment instructions
* Escalate where the declared owner cannot explain the business

Reading references: FATF and Egmont Group, Concealment of Beneficial Ownership (2018); FATF, Guidance on Beneficial Ownership of Legal Persons (2023)

## T03 Identity variation used to defeat counting

Area: Nominee director network

The same person can be filed under different spellings, with or without middle names, with initials, or with a slightly different month of birth. Each variation looks like a new individual to a system that counts exact names.

Indicators

* Several officer records share a surname, a month and year of birth and near identical forenames
* Spelling differences are small and inconsistent across filings
* The variations all lead to companies at the same addresses

Why it matters: Counting appointments by exact name undercounts prolific nominees. Entity resolution on name and date of birth is needed before any volume rule is applied.

Reviewer actions

* Resolve officer identities before applying appointment thresholds
* Use identity verification status from the registrar where it is available
* Record every spelling variant in the case file

Reading references: Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023; FATF, Guidance on Beneficial Ownership of Legal Persons (2023)

## T04 Mass registration addresses and company factories

Area: Mass registration address

Some addresses host hundreds or thousands of companies that exist only on paper. They are created in batches, used briefly as vehicles for fraud or laundering, and abandoned.

Indicators

* A very large number of companies share one registered office
* Most companies at the address are dissolved within two years or never file accounts
* Directors are predominantly resident overseas
* Incorporations arrive in waves within a few days of each other
* A handful of directors account for most companies at the address

Why it matters: A registered office gives a shell an appearance of UK substance. A shared address with a high share of disposable companies is one of the strongest network level indicators available from public data.

Reviewer actions

* Treat the address as a risk factor for every company registered there
* Check whether the business has any real presence at the address
* Report suspected false addresses to the registrar

Reading references: HM Treasury and Home Office, National Risk Assessment of Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing (2020); FATF and Egmont Group, Concealment of Beneficial Ownership (2018)

## T05 Distinguishing formation agents from company factories

Area: Mass registration address

Trust and company service providers, accountants and virtual offices legitimately host many companies. Their client companies live for years, file accounts on time and have diverse, mostly local directors.

Indicators

* Low share of early dissolutions at the address
* Accounts filed on time by most companies
* Many different directors, each with few companies
* Incorporation dates spread evenly over time

Why it matters: An address count alone produces large numbers of false alerts on legitimate agents. The behaviour of the companies at the address separates the two.

Reviewer actions

* Check that the service provider is supervised for anti money laundering purposes
* Use address behaviour, not address volume, to set risk
* Do not exit customers only because they use a shared office

Reading references: Money Laundering, Terrorist Financing and Transfer of Funds (Information on the Payer) Regulations 2017; Joint Money Laundering Steering Group, Guidance for the UK Financial Sector

## T06 Registered office abuse and inappropriate addresses

Area: Mass registration address

Companies have been registered at addresses without the knowledge of the occupier, or at addresses where documents cannot be delivered. Recent reforms require every company to keep an appropriate registered office address and give the registrar power to change it.

Indicators

* Residents or businesses at the address deny any link to the company
* The address is a mail drop with no ability to acknowledge documents
* The registrar has moved the company to a default address

Why it matters: An inappropriate registered office is now itself a compliance failure and can lead to strike off.

Reviewer actions

* Verify the registered office independently
* Check the filing history for address changes imposed by the registrar

Reading references: Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023

## T07 Circular ownership structures

Area: Circular ownership

In a circular structure each company is recorded as controlled by another company in the same group, and the chain eventually returns to where it started. No natural person appears at any point.

Indicators

* Company A lists company B as its controller while B, directly or through others, lists A
* No individual person with significant control anywhere in the group
* The same director sits on every company in the loop

Why it matters: A loop of declared control cannot be a true statement of ultimate ownership. It either conceals the real owner or reflects false filings, and both justify escalation.

Reviewer actions

* Refuse to accept the structure as evidence of beneficial ownership
* Require the customer to name the natural persons who ultimately benefit
* Report the discrepancy to the registrar

Reading references: FATF and Egmont Group, Concealment of Beneficial Ownership (2018); Companies Act 2006, Part 21A (persons with significant control)

## T08 Cross border loops through offshore entities

Area: Circular ownership

A loop is harder to see when one link runs through an entity registered offshore. The UK register shows an overseas corporate controller, and only foreign or leaked records show that the overseas entity is in turn held by another UK company in the same group.

Indicators

* A corporate controller registered in a secrecy jurisdiction
* Leak data names a UK company as shareholder of that offshore entity
* The UK shareholder is itself controlled by the first company or its subsidiaries

Why it matters: Each national register looks unremarkable in isolation. The loop only appears when the datasets are joined.

Reviewer actions

* Join domestic control records with overseas and leaked ownership data
* Ask for certified registers of members for the offshore entity

Reading references: FATF and Egmont Group, Concealment of Beneficial Ownership (2018); FATF, Guidance on Beneficial Ownership of Legal Persons (2023)

## T09 Layering through long chains of corporate owners

Area: Circular ownership

Chains of several corporate owners placed one above another add distance between an asset and its owner. Each added layer raises the cost of tracing without any commercial purpose.

Indicators

* Three or more corporate layers above an operating company
* Layers registered in different countries
* Layers with no employees, premises or accounts

Why it matters: Complexity without a commercial rationale is a recognised indicator of concealment.

Reviewer actions

* Ask for the commercial reason for each layer
* Obtain a full structure chart signed by a senior officer

Reading references: FATF and Egmont Group, Concealment of Beneficial Ownership (2018); Joint Money Laundering Steering Group, Guidance for the UK Financial Sector

## T10 Indirect ownership or control by designated persons

Area: Sanctions proximity

UK financial sanctions apply not only to a designated person but also to any entity that the person owns or controls, directly or indirectly. Ownership means holding more than 50 percent of the shares or voting rights or the right to appoint or remove a majority of the board. Control also exists where it is reasonable to expect that the person can ensure the affairs of the entity are conducted in line with their wishes.

Indicators

* A designated person is a person with significant control or director of the company
* A designated person controls an entity that in turn controls the company
* The chain between the designated person and the company is made of majority holdings

Why it matters: An entity that is owned or controlled by a designated person is treated as subject to the same asset freeze even if it is not named on the list.

Reviewer actions

* Freeze and do not deal with funds where ownership or control is established
* Report to OFSI as required
* Seek legal advice where control is suspected but not established

Reading references: OFSI, UK Financial Sanctions General Guidance; Sanctions and Anti Money Laundering Act 2018

## T11 Aggregation of holdings and the fifty percent rule

Area: Sanctions proximity

Under the United States rule, an entity owned 50 percent or more in aggregate by one or more blocked persons is itself blocked. UK guidance takes a different view of aggregation: holdings of different designated persons are not simply added together unless there is a joint arrangement or one controls the rights of the other.

Indicators

* Two or more listed parties each hold a minority stake
* Minority stakes that together exceed half of the shares
* Shareholders that act together under an agreement

Why it matters: The same structure can be blocked under one regime and not under another, so exposure has to be assessed regime by regime.

Reviewer actions

* Compute effective ownership along every path from a listed party
* Assess each applicable sanctions regime separately

Reading references: OFAC, Guidance on Entities Owned by Persons Whose Property Is Blocked (50 Percent Rule); OFSI, UK Financial Sanctions General Guidance

## T12 Name variants and transliteration in screening

Area: Sanctions proximity

Names written in other scripts can be rendered in the Latin alphabet in many ways. Lists publish some aliases, but a registry filing may use a spelling that is on no list.

Indicators

* A name differs from a list entry only by vowels, doubled letters or common letter substitutions
* Surname and forename order is reversed
* Date of birth agrees with the list entry

Why it matters: Exact matching misses designated persons. Fuzzy matching without secondary identifiers produces unmanageable false alerts. Both name similarity and date of birth are needed.

Reviewer actions

* Screen with transliteration aware matching
* Use date of birth and nationality to confirm or discount a name match
* Record why each potential match was confirmed or discounted

Reading references: OFSI, UK Financial Sanctions General Guidance; Joint Money Laundering Steering Group, Guidance for the UK Financial Sector

## T13 Handling name only matches

Area: Sanctions proximity

A match on name alone, with no date of birth or other identifier to confirm it, is a potential match and not a finding. Common names produce many such matches against innocent people.

Indicators

* The source record has no date of birth
* The list entry has no date of birth
* Year of birth agrees but month does not

Why it matters: Acting on an unconfirmed match harms an innocent customer. Ignoring it risks a sanctions breach. The correct response is review by a trained person.

Reviewer actions

* Route name only matches to manual review
* Seek additional identifiers before any adverse action
* Do not describe a potential match as a confirmed designation

Reading references: OFSI, UK Financial Sanctions General Guidance; Joint Money Laundering Steering Group, Guidance for the UK Financial Sector

## T14 Corporate controllers registered in secrecy jurisdictions

Area: Opaque offshore ownership

A company may record another legal entity as its person with significant control only when that entity is itself subject to equivalent disclosure, for example because it keeps its own control register or is listed on a qualifying market. A private company from a secrecy jurisdiction does not normally meet that test, so the filing should have looked through it to the individuals behind it.

Indicators

* The only registered controller is a corporate entity from a secrecy jurisdiction
* The legal form given is an international business company or similar
* No natural person appears anywhere in the declared chain

Why it matters: Recording an offshore company as the controller ends the public trail at the border. It is one of the most common ways the control register is defeated.

Reviewer actions

* Require identification of the natural persons behind the offshore entity
* Obtain certified ownership documents from the offshore registered agent
* Report the discrepancy between the register and the information obtained

Reading references: Companies Act 2006, Part 21A (persons with significant control); FATF and Egmont Group, Concealment of Beneficial Ownership (2018); FATF, Guidance on Beneficial Ownership of Legal Persons (2023)

## T15 Statements that there is no registrable person

Area: Opaque offshore ownership

A company can state that it has no person with significant control. That is true for companies with widely spread ownership, but the statement is also used to avoid naming a controller.

Indicators

* A small private company with one director states that nobody controls it
* The statement is combined with corporate officers or overseas directors

Why it matters: For a closely held company the statement is implausible and should be challenged.

Reviewer actions

* Obtain the register of members and compare holdings with the statement
* Challenge the statement where a single shareholder holds more than a quarter

Reading references: Companies Act 2006, Part 21A (persons with significant control); FATF, Guidance on Beneficial Ownership of Legal Persons (2023)

## T16 Hidden controllers revealed by leak data

Area: Opaque offshore ownership

Leaked records from offshore service providers sometimes name the shareholders, directors or beneficiaries of an entity that the official register leaves blank. Joining those records to the control register can reveal a person behind an opaque chain.

Indicators

* An offshore controller has named officers in leak data
* Those officers do not appear anywhere in the UK register for the company

Why it matters: Leak data can close a gap in the declared chain. It is also dated, incomplete and unverified, and appearing in it is not evidence of wrongdoing since offshore entities have lawful uses.

Reviewer actions

* Treat leak data as a lead to verify, never as proof
* Ask the customer to confirm or explain the relationship
* Record the source and date of the leaked record

Reading references: ICIJ, Offshore Leaks Database notes on use of the data; FATF and Egmont Group, Concealment of Beneficial Ownership (2018)

## T17 Legitimate foreign parent structures

Area: Opaque offshore ownership

Subsidiaries of listed or regulated foreign groups also show a corporate controller and no natural person. They differ from concealment structures because the parent is subject to public disclosure in its home market and the subsidiary files full accounts.

Indicators

* Parent is listed or regulated in a jurisdiction with public ownership disclosure
* Subsidiary files audited or full accounts
* Directors are employees of the group

Why it matters: Treating every corporate controller as suspicious floods reviewers with alerts on ordinary multinational groups.

Reviewer actions

* Verify the listing or regulatory status of the parent
* Apply standard rather than enhanced due diligence where the parent is transparent

Reading references: Companies Act 2006, Part 21A (persons with significant control); Joint Money Laundering Steering Group, Guidance for the UK Financial Sector

## T18 Phoenix companies

Area: Burst incorporation and phoenix activity

A phoenix company is created to carry on the business of a company that has been dissolved or has failed, with the same people behind it and a near identical name, leaving creditors and tax debts with the old company.

Indicators

* A new company is incorporated within weeks of the dissolution of a similarly named company
* Same director and same address as the dissolved company
* The dissolved company never filed accounts
* The pattern repeats several times

Why it matters: Reusing the name of a company that went into insolvent liquidation is restricted, and directors of dissolved companies can be investigated and disqualified.

Reviewer actions

* Review the history of dissolved companies linked to the director
* Check for unpaid creditors and tax debts of the predecessor
* Consider a report to the Insolvency Service

Reading references: Insolvency Act 1986, section 216; Company Directors Disqualification Act 1986

## T19 Burst incorporation of shell batches

Area: Burst incorporation and phoenix activity

Batches of companies are formed on the same day or within a few days by the same person at the same address, used for a short campaign such as invoice fraud or mule accounts, and then left to be struck off.

Indicators

* Many companies incorporated within days by one director
* Names that share a common stem
* No accounts filed and dissolution within eighteen months

Why it matters: A batch gives an offender a supply of fresh bank accounts and trading names that can be discarded as each one is detected.

Reviewer actions

* Link all members of the batch in one case
* Check whether accounts opened for batch members show similar activity

Reading references: HM Treasury and Home Office, National Risk Assessment of Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing (2020); FATF and Egmont Group, Concealment of Beneficial Ownership (2018)

## T20 Legitimate batches such as property special purpose vehicles

Area: Burst incorporation and phoenix activity

Developers and investors lawfully create several companies at once, often one per property or project. These companies stay on the register for years and file accounts.

Indicators

* Batch members remain active for many years
* Accounts are filed for each company
* Directors are resident locally

Why it matters: Same day incorporation is not suspicious by itself. Abandonment is what separates a disposable batch from a project structure.

Reviewer actions

* Confirm the commercial purpose of the batch
* Apply standard due diligence where the companies show real activity

Reading references: Joint Money Laundering Steering Group, Guidance for the UK Financial Sector

## T21 Customer due diligence on ownership and control

Area: General guidance

A regulated firm must identify the beneficial owner of a corporate customer, take reasonable measures to verify that identity and understand the ownership and control structure. The public control register cannot be relied on as the only source.

Indicators

* The declared structure cannot be reconciled with documents obtained from the customer
* The customer is reluctant to explain who owns it

Why it matters: Understanding the structure is a legal requirement, and enhanced measures are required wherever the risk is higher.

Reviewer actions

* Obtain and verify beneficial ownership information from the customer
* Apply enhanced due diligence in higher risk situations
* Keep the structure under review during the relationship

Reading references: Money Laundering, Terrorist Financing and Transfer of Funds (Information on the Payer) Regulations 2017; Joint Money Laundering Steering Group, Guidance for the UK Financial Sector

## T22 Reporting discrepancies in the control register

Area: General guidance

Where a regulated firm finds a material discrepancy between the control register and the beneficial ownership information it has obtained, it must report the discrepancy to the registrar.

Indicators

* The register names a different controller from the one the firm identified
* The register shows no controller where the firm identified one

Why it matters: Discrepancy reports are how the public register is corrected, and failing to report is a regulatory breach.

Reviewer actions

* File a discrepancy report with the registrar
* Record the report in the customer file

Reading references: Money Laundering, Terrorist Financing and Transfer of Funds (Information on the Payer) Regulations 2017

## T23 Suspicious activity reporting and tipping off

Area: General guidance

Where there is knowledge or suspicion of money laundering, a report must be made to the National Crime Agency through the nominated officer. The customer must not be told that a report has been made.

Indicators

* Findings that cannot be explained after reasonable enquiry
* Customer explanations that conflict with the evidence

Why it matters: Failure to disclose in the regulated sector and tipping off are criminal offences.

Reviewer actions

* Escalate to the nominated officer
* Do not disclose the suspicion or the report to the customer

Reading references: Proceeds of Crime Act 2002, Part 7

## T24 Limits of automated findings

Area: General guidance

Automated detection produces indicators of risk. It does not establish that an offence has taken place. Registry data contains errors, entity resolution can merge different people, and leak data is unverified.

Indicators

* A finding rests on a name only match
* A finding rests on a single unresolved corporate link
* The underlying filing is several years old

Why it matters: Adverse action on an unverified finding can harm an innocent person and expose the firm to complaints and legal challenge.

Reviewer actions

* Have a trained reviewer confirm every finding before action
* Record the evidence relied on and the decision reached
* Feed confirmed and rejected findings back into tuning

Reading references: Joint Money Laundering Steering Group, Guidance for the UK Financial Sector; FATF, Guidance on Beneficial Ownership of Legal Persons (2023)

## T25 Opaque partnership forms

Area: Opaque offshore ownership

Limited partnerships whose partners are offshore companies have been used in large laundering schemes because, historically, they disclosed little about who controlled them.

Indicators

* Partners are corporate entities registered in secrecy jurisdictions
* The partnership has no evident business in the country of registration
* The same corporate partners recur across many partnerships

Why it matters: Partnership forms were for a long time outside the control register, which made them attractive as anonymous vehicles.

Reviewer actions

* Identify the natural persons behind each corporate partner
* Check whether the partnership now reports its controllers

Reading references: HM Treasury and Home Office, National Risk Assessment of Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing (2020); FATF and Egmont Group, Concealment of Beneficial Ownership (2018)
